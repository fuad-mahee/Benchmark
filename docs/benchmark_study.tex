\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Two published methods claim to detect and repair \emph{glitch tokens}: vocabulary
entries so under-trained that they derail a language model's output. \gp{} reports
an average repair rate of $50.06\%$; \gc{} reports $86.88\%$ and positions itself
as a more-than-30-point improvement over \gp{}. Neither claim has been
independently verified, and the comparison between them is constructed by
importing numbers across two different experimental setups.

This study re-executes both methods under a single controlled protocol on a model
both papers use (\texttt{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1}), regenerating every quantity
from scratch rather than citing published values.

Our central methodological result concerns the \emph{denominator}. A repair rate
is a fraction of a glitch-token population, and we show that this population is
not a property of the model but of the probing prompt. Holding the model,
precision, decoding, and vocabulary filter fixed, we count $988$ glitch tokens
under the prompt the papers describe and $2{,}552$ under the prompt \gc{}'s own
released code uses. A post-hoc factorial over the archived generations leaves
$99.0\%$ of that gap unexplained by the two factors we can measure directly
(generation budget, match rule), i.e.\ attributable to the prompt \emph{wording}
as a residual, and shows a mechanism: under \gc{}'s template, $42.7\%$ of the
tokens classified as glitchy produce nothing but whitespace, consistent with the
template's trailing newline driving the model into a newline-repetition attractor.
The two papers themselves worked with populations of $2{,}779$ and $2{,}539$
tokens for this model, and we show that \gc{}'s reported \gp{} figures are \gp{}'s
rates rescaled onto \gc{}'s denominator rather than measurements.

That population is not only a denominator. \gc{} consults it at inference time as
the gate that makes its repair ``lossless'': the adapter is disabled unless the
input contains a token from the set. But the set is what the probe produced, and
\gc{}'s own published set for this model contains \texttt{and}, \texttt{for},
\texttt{with}, \texttt{by} and the apostrophe --- so the gate opens on $57\%$ of
ordinary sentences, on the evaluation's own boilerplate, and on every one of the
$6{,}079$ examples we ran through the authors' own released adapter. Losslessness
is therefore not delivered by the mechanism the paper credits for it; \gc{}'s own
capability table confirms this, since a truly disabled adapter would leave those
scores bit-identical and it does not.

Where the methods' own claims are testable, we report what we found rather than
what a reproduction study is expected to find. Loading \gc{}'s released weights
reproduces its headline repair rate to within a single token ($2{,}408$ against a
published $2{,}407$ of $2{,}539$), and an earlier version of this study reported a
shortfall on that claim which we now attribute to our own training rather than to
the method. For \gp{}, whose code was never released, two implementation choices
the paper does not state move its repair rate by $7.8\times$ --- a range that
brackets its own published figure, leaving the claim under-determined rather than
refuted.

We subjected our own implementation to an adversarial external audit before
reporting anything. It found two errors that invalidated specific conclusions and
several conclusions stated more strongly than their evidence supported. Both
errors were corrected, the affected experiments re-run, and both the
pre-correction and post-correction numbers are reported --- because the gap
between them measures how sensitive these methods are to implementation choices
that neither paper specifies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The phenomenon}

A language model does not read characters; it reads \emph{tokens} drawn from a
fixed vocabulary fitted before training. Some of those tokens occur almost never
in the training corpus. Their embedding vectors are barely updated from
initialisation, and the model has no coherent representation of what they mean.
When such a token appears in a prompt, the model's behaviour degrades in ways that
look like malfunction rather than ordinary error: asked to repeat the string
\texttt{Azalera}, one recent model answers \texttt{saysay}; asked for the length
of \texttt{Kapunoang}, it answers \texttt{3}. These are \emph{glitch tokens}.

They matter twice over. As a reliability problem, a user who pastes text
containing one receives nonsense with no warning. As a security problem, they
occupy a region of representation space the model never learned to control, and
have been used as a lever for prompt injection and jailbreak attacks.

\subsection{The two methods}

\paragraph{\gp{} (Zhang et al., ASE 2024) --- detection and repair.}
Detection reads the model's internals: label a random $10\%$ of the vocabulary by
brute force, extract activation features for those tokens from a band of late
transformer layers, reduce with PCA, fit a support-vector classifier, apply it to
the remaining $90\%$, and re-validate every predicted positive with the brute-force
test. Repair edits activations at inference time: neurons reliably active for
normal tokens but under-active for a glitch token are amplified by a factor
$\beta$; neurons reliably silent for normal tokens but abnormally firing are
suppressed by a factor $\alpha$. No weights change.

\paragraph{\gc{} (Fan, Li \& Li, AAAI 2026) --- repair only.}
Attaches LoRA adapters ($<0.1\%$ additional parameters) to the same components of
the same layer band and trains them on (glitch token $\rightarrow$ correct answer)
pairs. The adapters are \emph{gated}: a switch $\lambda$ enables the branch only
when the input contains a token from a known glitch set $G$, so clean inputs are
processed by the mathematically unmodified base model. This gate is the basis of
the paper's ``lossless'' claim.

\subsection{Why these particular claims require verification}
\label{sec:why}

Reproduction work is often motivated in general terms. Here the motivation is
specific and checkable: four problems are visible in the published material
\emph{before} any experiment is run.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{The comparison spans incompatible populations.} \gc{}'s headline is a
comparison against \gp{}, but the two papers worked with different glitch-token
sets for the same model. For \texttt{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1}, \gp{}'s own tables
imply a population of $2{,}779$ tokens; three independent derivations agree:
$1873/0.6741 = 2778.5$ from detection, $359/0.1292 = 2778.6$ and
$1045/0.3760 = 2779.3$ from repair. \gc{} states $2{,}539$. A repair rate is a
fraction of this population, so the two rates are fractions of different things.

\item \textbf{The reported competitor counts are derived, not measured.} \gc{}
states that ``the \gp{} results are taken from their original paper.'' The
\emph{rates} are. The \emph{token counts} are not: for all five shared models,
\gc{}'s reported \gp{} repaired-token count equals \gp{}'s \emph{rate} multiplied
by \gc{}'s \emph{own} denominator, not the count \gp{} reports
(Table~\ref{tab:rescale}). For Mistral the value $956$ does not appear anywhere in
\gp{}'s paper, which reports $1{,}045$.

The two boldface disagreements are small but they are not noise, and they are the
part of this table that is hardest to explain any other way. If \gc{} had quoted
\gp{}'s rates, its rates would equal \gp{}'s in all five rows. They do not: the
rows where the rescaled count required rounding are exactly the rows where the
rate moved, and it moved to whatever the \emph{rounded count} divided by \gc{}'s
own $|G|$ gives ($956/2539 = 37.65\%$; $3188/5985 = 53.27\%$). The reported average
for the \gp{} column follows the same pattern: $50.08\%$, the mean of the five
recomputed rates, against \gp{}'s own published average of $50.06\%$.

To be fair to the authors: the rate is carried over faithfully, and rescaling is a
defensible way to place two rates on a common axis. Two things are nevertheless
wrong with it as presented. The counts are labelled as taken from the original
paper when they are computed; and the computation silently assumes that \gp{}'s
rate transfers to a \emph{different set} of tokens. Section~\ref{sec:rq1} shows
these are not merely different-sized populations but different token sets, so the
assumption is substantive and untested. This is exactly the failure that fixing
the denominator prevents.

\item \textbf{Neither paper reports variance.} \gp{} depends on a random $10\%$
vocabulary sample; \gc{} on a random adapter initialisation and batch order. Both
report single numbers. Without a spread there is no way to tell a real difference
from run-to-run noise.

\item \textbf{\gc{} appears to evaluate on its training data.} The paper builds a
dataset from the identified glitch tokens, trains the adapters on it, and reports
repair over the glitch tokens. No held-out split is described. If the training and
evaluation populations coincide, the headline number contains memorisation.
\end{enumerate}

Separately, \gp{} released no code, and the constants of its adaptive repair rule
are never given numerically --- a reproducibility question in its own right
(Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

\subsection{What this study is, and what it can conclude}
\label{sec:epistemics}

This is a reproduction and differential evaluation, not a new method. Being
precise about its limits matters, because overclaiming is the characteristic
hazard of such work.

When our measurement disagrees with a published number there are always exactly
two explanations: the claim does not hold, or our reconstruction differs from what
the authors did. Only two instruments distinguish them.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Anchoring.} If, under the authors' own conditions, our pipeline
reproduces a quantity they published, our reconstruction is demonstrably faithful
in that respect and the remaining disagreements become informative. We achieve
this for \gc{}: our census under their released code's protocol recovers $2{,}537$
of their $2{,}539$ published glitch tokens (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
\item \textbf{Refusal to attribute.} Where no anchor exists --- notably \gp{} ---
a disagreement is reported as ``we could not reproduce $X$ from the published
description,'' never as ``$X$ is false.'' Section~\ref{sec:threats} states
explicitly which findings are anchored and which are not.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research questions}
% =====================================================================

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5cm,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1 --- Population] How many glitch tokens does the model have, and how much
does that number depend on the protocol used to probe it? \emph{This precedes
everything else: it is the denominator of every repair rate.}
\item[RQ2 --- \gp{} detection] Does detection reproduce its claimed precision,
recall and F1, and how much do these vary across random samples?
\item[RQ3 --- \gp{} repair] Does repair reproduce its claimed rate; is its
parameterisation justified by its own behaviour; and what does it cost normal
tokens?
\item[RQ4 --- \gc{} repair] Does the LoRA repair reproduce its claimed rate, and
does it hold on glitch tokens the adapter never trained on?
\item[RQ5 --- Side effects] What do the methods cost in inference speed?
\end{description}

\section{Experimental design}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Subject model}

All experiments use \texttt{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1}, chosen for three reasons:
both papers use it, so both claim sets apply; it is where the papers disagree most
sharply (\gc{} reports $94.80\%$ repair while attributing $37.65\%$ to \gp{}); and
it is ungated, so any reader can reproduce this without access approvals.

Restricting to one model is a deliberate depth-for-breadth trade. Every claim
below is a claim about this model; Section~\ref{sec:threats} addresses
generalisation, and the infrastructure is model-agnostic so additional models are
a matter of configuration and compute.

\subsection{Environment}

Single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48\,GB), fp16, native Windows, PyTorch 2.6 / CUDA 12.4.
The original papers used $2\times$A100 (\gp{}) and an H200 (\gc{}'s speed table).
Because absolute wall-clock times are not comparable across hardware,
\textbf{every timing claim here is a ratio measured within our own environment},
never an absolute duration compared against a published one.

\section{Methodology}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Ground truth: the census}
\label{sec:census-method}

Both papers define a glitch token \emph{operationally}, by the same test: prompt
the model to repeat the token verbatim at temperature $0$; if it fails, the token
is glitchy. We adopt the same definition but apply it exhaustively to the whole
vocabulary, so the result is a census rather than an estimate.

\paragraph{Filtering, and a cautionary result.}
Some entries cannot meaningfully be tested and are excluded first, following the
procedure of Land \& Bartolo (2024) that both papers adopt: control tokens; tokens
that do not decode to valid text; and \emph{unreachable} tokens, whose decoded
string re-encodes to a different id and which therefore can never arise from real
input.

The reachability test is subtler than it appears, and our first implementation got
it wrong instructively. A naive decode-then-re-encode check classified $12{,}385$
tokens ($39\%$ of the vocabulary) as unreachable, because SentencePiece attaches
leading-space semantics to a string in isolation that it does not attach in
context. The correct procedure --- taken from \gc{}'s own released filter, itself
following Land \& Bartolo --- performs the round trip behind a fixed one-token
prefix, testing the token in context. This yields $257$ filtered tokens, matching
the scale both papers report. We record this because the filtering step is
load-bearing and a plausible implementation of it is off by two orders of
magnitude.

\paragraph{Prompt construction.}
The token under test is inserted as its \emph{raw vocabulary id}, spliced between
two pre-tokenised template halves, rather than by string concatenation followed by
re-tokenisation. This guarantees the model receives exactly the token being tested
(concatenation can re-tokenise into a different sequence) and makes every prompt
the same length, which permits exact batching.

\subsection{Two protocols, and why both are necessary}
\label{sec:two-protocols}

While implementing the census we found that \gc{}'s released code does not use the
prompt its paper describes. Two protocols therefore exist and we implement both.

This is not pedantry. \Pcode{} exists so our numbers can be \emph{anchored} to the

behaves differently than claimed'' from ``we probed it differently.''

\subsection{Reimplementation of \gp{}}
\label{sec:gp-reimpl}

With no public code, \gp{} was reconstructed from Algorithm 1 (detection),
Algorithm 2 (repair), Equations 7--12, and its stated hyperparameters:
$\gamma = 0.1$ sampling rate, $P = 75$ PCA components, polynomial SVM with $C=1$
and degree $3$, threshold $m=1$, key layers $19$--$28$.

Three aspects are \emph{under-specified in the paper} and therefore required a
choice on our part. A reader must be able to tell our decisions from the authors',
so all three are recorded, and two are measured rather than assumed.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Which tensor is adjusted.} The gated MLP computes a gate signal
$\sigma(Z_1)$ and a data signal $Z_2$ and multiplies them. The paper's Figure 5
shows a ``Modified MLP gate'' \emph{and} a ``Modified MLP data'', i.e.\ the two are
adjusted separately before multiplication; we follow that reading, and report the
alternative (adjusting the product) as an ablation, because our own first
implementation took it and the difference turns out to be large.
\item \textbf{Which sequence positions are affected.} The paper never states the
position at which activations are read or the correction applied. We default to
the position of the token under test, which matches ``the activation states of
glitch tokens''; on this reading the correction fires only during the prefill
pass, since that is the only pass in which the token is present. The alternative
--- read and correct at the last position of every forward pass, so the correction
continues to fire at each decoding step long after the glitch token has been
consumed --- is reported as an ablation and labelled ``all positions''.
Section~\ref{sec:rq3} shows the choice changes repair rate several-fold; it is as
consequential as the missing constants, and unlike them it is invisible to a
reader, since nothing in the paper signals that a choice exists.
\item \textbf{The adaptive constants.} Equations 11--12 define
$\beta = k_1\Delta_\uparrow + b_1$ and $\alpha = k_2\Delta_\downarrow + b_2$, but
$k_1, b_1, k_2, b_2$ are never given; the paper says only that they are ``derived
through an adaptive process tailored to the specific dynamics of each model'' and
mentions a ``range restriction'' that is likewise unspecified. The ``set of
default values'' the text says is provided does not appear in the paper. We use
identity constants with an explicit clamp; this is \emph{our} choice and, as
Section~\ref{sec:rq3} shows, a degenerate one, which is why we evaluate the
mechanism by parameter sweep instead.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reimplementation of \gc{}}

\gc{} released code, which we treat as the specification of record wherever the
paper is ambiguous. Our implementation matches it on LoRA rank $4$ and scaling
$4$; adapters on the MLP gate and data projections of layers $19$--$28$ only;
frozen base weights; loss on the answer span only; and the authors' exact target
format (the decoded token, unstripped, in single quotes). We also adopt their
optimisation settings --- $15$ epochs, learning rate $1\times 10^{-4}$, effective
batch $512$, linear schedule with $10\%$ warmup, gradient clipping at $1.0$ ---
so that a hyperparameter mismatch cannot be offered as an explanation for any
difference in outcome.

\paragraph{The gate.} We implement $\lambda$ as the paper and the authors' code
define it: a per-example flag, $1$ when the input contains a token from the known
set $G$ and $0$ otherwise, multiplying the LoRA branch's contribution. Two
consequences follow and both are measured rather than assumed. First,
``lossless'' is a property of \emph{the gate}, not of the adapter: with the gate
active a clean prompt is processed by the unmodified base model by construction.
Second, the branch is still \emph{computed} when $\lambda = 0$ --- true of the
authors' implementation as well --- so the gate removes the behavioural effect but
not the arithmetic cost, which is why it does not exempt the method from an
inference-speed penalty.

\paragraph{The held-out split (our addition).} The paper trains and evaluates on
the same population. We split the glitch set $80/20$, train on the $80\%$, and
evaluate both halves. This is the single most informative addition this study
makes to \gc{}'s own evaluation, because it separates two things the paper's
design conflates: repairing glitch tokens in general, and memorising the specific
ones shown during training.

\paragraph{Leakage control.} A token is only ``unseen'' if the adapter never
encountered its id. An id can appear inside a training \emph{sequence} without
being that sequence's subject --- as ordinary context, or because another token's
decoded text re-encodes through it. We scan every training sequence and exclude
from the held-out set any token whose id appears in one. Without this control the
held-out result is optimistic in a way invisible from the split file alone; in our
data it removed $10$ of the $197$ nominally held-out tokens under \Ppaper{} and
$35$ of $510$ under \Pcode{}.

\subsection{Metrics, stated with their denominators}

Ambiguity about denominators is the specific defect this study exists to correct,
so every metric is defined against an explicit population.
\begin{align*}
\text{repair rate} &= \frac{\#\{\text{glitch tokens repeating correctly with the method active}\}}{|\text{our glitch census for this model under this protocol}|}\\[0.3em]
\text{collateral breakage} &= \frac{\#\{\text{normal tokens that stop repeating correctly with the method active}\}}{|\text{sampled normal tokens}|}\\[0.3em]
\text{recall} &= \frac{|\hat G \cap G|}{|G|},\qquad
\text{precision} = \frac{|\hat G \cap G|}{|\hat G|}
\end{align*}

\paragraph{The three denominators, stated once.} A study whose thesis is that
denominators are under-reported cannot use three of them silently, so they are
named here and every reported rate below says which it uses.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.6cm}}
\toprule
Symbol & Size (\Ppaper{}) & Used for \\
\midrule
census        & $988$ & the population itself (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}); the
                        denominator of \gp{}'s and \gc{}'s published rates \\
eval half     & $494$ & \gp{} repair (Table~\ref{tab:implchoice}). The census is
                        split in two: one half fits the adaptive $\alpha,\beta$,
                        both variants are scored on the other, so the comparison
                        is not stacked in the adaptive variant's favour \\
grid sample   & $500$ & each cell of the $\alpha \times \beta$ grid and the
                        $m$-sweep, drawn from the census \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The eval half and the grid sample are different draws, so the same
configuration can differ slightly between tables: at the paper's
$(\alpha,\beta) = (4, 1.5)$ we measure $6.48\%$ ($32/494$) and $6.80\%$
($34/500$). That $0.3$-point difference is the sampling noise floor for every
contrast in Section~\ref{sec:rq3}. \gc{}'s rates use its own split sizes, given in
Table~\ref{tab:gc}.

Collateral breakage deserves comment: \emph{neither paper reports it}. A method
that repairs glitch tokens by degrading normal ones is not obviously an
improvement, and the quantity is cheap to measure, so we measure it in every
configuration.

\subsection{Statistical treatment}

Every sampled quantity is run at three seeds and reported as mean $\pm$ sample
standard deviation. Three seeds bound variance only loosely and we do not treat
them as more; where we describe spread we give the interval rather than an
adjective. It is nevertheless strictly more than the papers report, which is a
single number with no spread.

\subsection{Reproducibility infrastructure}

A study whose purpose is to check other people's numbers has no standing unless
its own can be checked. Four properties are built in.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Traceability.} Every result file records timestamp, git commit,
\emph{whether the working tree was modified at the time}, and the versions of
PyTorch, Transformers, PEFT and scikit-learn. The dirty-tree flag was added after
the audit found artefacts attributed to commits that could not have produced them:
a commit hash alone is not provenance if the run was launched from an edited tree.
\item \textbf{Raw evidence retention.} Full generations are retained for every
probe and per-token outcomes for every repair evaluation, so headline numbers can
be recomputed from primary data rather than trusted. This retention is what made
the factorial decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:rq1} possible after the fact.
\item \textbf{Checkpointing with configuration guards.} Long sweeps checkpoint per
batch and resume exactly. Resumption is guarded by a configuration fingerprint: a
checkpoint written under different settings is refused rather than silently
extended. This guard was added after we caught a ``corrected'' parameter grid that
had silently resumed its own pre-correction checkpoint and was reporting stale
numbers under fresh metadata.
\item \textbf{Externalised configuration.} All experimental settings live in YAML;
nothing experiment-relevant is hard-coded.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\section{Internal validation: an adversarial audit of our own implementation}
\label{sec:audit}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Why this was done, and why it is reported}

A reproduction study that audits only other people's work is not credible. Before
reporting any result we submitted the implementation to an independent adversarial
review, instructed to treat all code, documentation and conclusions as unverified
claims. It checked faithfulness to the papers, correctness of the code, integrity
of the stored results, and whether each conclusion was supported at the strength
stated.

It found two errors serious enough to invalidate specific conclusions, several
correctness defects, and a number of overstated conclusions. All are reported
here. This is deliberate: the pre- and post-correction numbers differ, and
\emph{the size of that difference is itself a result}, because it measures how
sensitive these methods are to choices the papers do not pin down.

\subsection{The two material errors}

\paragraph{E1: \gp{} repair modified the wrong tensor.}
Our first implementation adjusted the already-multiplied product of the gate and
data streams rather than the two streams separately. Adding $\beta$ to a product
is not adding $\beta$ to its factors. The consequence was not merely numerical:
the neuron-selection criterion (``active in $>99\%$ of normal tokens''), applied
to the product, selected $0$--$4$ neurons per layer, leaving the amplification
factor $\beta$ with almost nothing to act on. Our pre-correction conclusion that
``$\beta$ is inert'' was therefore partly an artefact of our own error. The
corrected implementation adjusts the streams separately; the previous behaviour is
retained as an explicit ablation rather than discarded (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

\paragraph{E2: \gc{}'s gate was absent.}
Our first implementation used an always-active adapter and disabled it globally
for one control. Repair rates on glitch prompts are unaffected (the gate would be
open for those prompts anyway), but every claim about \emph{clean-input} behaviour
--- losslessness, capability preservation, inference speed --- was measured on an
always-on adapter and therefore was not measuring \gc{}. The gate is now
implemented per example as specified.

\subsection{Correctness defects found and fixed}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=D\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Unseeded training.} Only the data split was seeded; adapter
initialisation and batch order were not, so the trained adapter was not
reproducible. All randomness is now seeded.
\item \textbf{Dropped gradients.} The optimiser stepped only on exact
gradient-accumulation boundaries, so each epoch's final partial window was
computed, never applied, and discarded. The authors' own script flushes this
window correctly; ours now does too.
\item \textbf{Adaptive parameters fitted on the evaluation set.} The adaptive
$\alpha,\beta$ were fitted on the same tokens they were scored on while the
fixed-value variant was not --- an unequal comparison. The glitch set is now split:
one half fits the adaptive parameters, both variants are scored on the other half.
\item \textbf{Deviation from Equation 10.} We had inserted an absolute value into
the denominator of the published signed ratio. The published form is restored and
the clamp is made explicit and configurable.
\item \textbf{Held-out leakage.} $10$ of $197$ nominally held-out tokens under
\Ppaper{} ($35$ of $510$ under \Pcode{}) had ids occurring inside training
sequences; these are now excluded.
\item \textbf{Training-target mismatch.} Our targets were stripped and unquoted;
the authors' are the unstripped decoded token in single quotes. A different target
teaches a different output format, so ours now matches theirs.
\item \textbf{Inconsistent attention kernel.} The census ran with SDPA attention
while detection requires eager (attention patterns are needed as features). The
two kernels disagree on a small fraction of greedy generations, which produced
spurious false positives that we had misattributed to fp16 non-determinism
(Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). The kernel is now controllable and recorded.
\item \textbf{Stale checkpoint reuse.} Fixed by the configuration-fingerprint
guard described above.
\item \textbf{An advertised experiment that never ran.} The sensitivity sweep over
the neuron-selection threshold $m$ was configured but never executed. It is now
implemented and reported.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusions we withdrew or weakened}
\label{sec:withdrawn}

The audit also challenged the \emph{strength} of several conclusions without
disputing the measurements. We re-adjudicated each against the stored artefacts;
the results sections state the corrected forms. In three cases the corrected
version is a stronger contribution than the original overstatement.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{``$\beta$ is inert''} --- withdrawn. Contradicted by our own grid at
$\alpha = 1$, and partly an artefact of E1. Replaced by a quantitative statement
of the two factors' unequal and interacting contributions
(Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
\item \textbf{``Pure protocol sensitivity'' attributed to generation length} ---
the umbrella claim survives and strengthens, but \emph{the stated cause was
wrong}. A post-hoc factorial over the archived generations shows generation length
accounts for $0.06\%$ of the gap and prompt wording for $99.0\%$
(Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). We had named the one variable that turned out to be
inert.
\item \textbf{``Unimplementable as published''} --- withdrawn, replaced by ``the
published adaptive calibration is unrecoverable,'' which is what the evidence
supports. We further stopped citing our identity-constant result as the method's
performance, because that configuration is degenerate (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
\item \textbf{``$100\%$ precision fails because of fp16 non-determinism''} ---
the measurement survives, the cause was wrong, and the framing was unfair to the
paper. The divergence is a deterministic attention-kernel mismatch between our own
scripts (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).
\item \textbf{``Losslessness requires a detector at inference''} --- corrected
twice. The gate is a membership lookup against a known set, not a learned
detector; that was the first correction. The second is larger and went the other
way. Having implemented the gate, we then reported that \gc{}'s losslessness claim
was \emph{supported}, on the strength of a clean-input result of $99.6\%$ under
\Pcode{}. Re-reading the stored artefact shows the gate never closed in that run
--- it recorded $\lambda = 1$ for all $975$ examples and $\lambda = 0$ for none ---
so the figure was the always-on number relabelled, which is the very error E2 was
raised to fix. The corrected finding is in Section~\ref{sec:rq4} and is a
substantive result about the method rather than about our code.
\item \textbf{``Both slowdown claims are withdrawn''} --- itself withdrawn. Our
corrected speed run reversed a large earlier finding, and we attributed the
reversal to a configuration change. The code shows no such change occurred
(Section~\ref{sec:rq5}). We now report no speed result at all.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A note on how these two were found.} Neither came from re-running
anything. Both came from checking a stored artefact against the sentence written
about it --- \texttt{gate\_stats} against ``losslessness is supported'',
\texttt{run\_speed.py} against ``the hooks now fire only at the token's
position''. The first audit caught errors in the \emph{code}; these are errors in
the \emph{reporting}, where the code did something defensible and the prose
described something else. They are harder to catch precisely because every number
involved is real and reproducible. We record this because the obvious lesson from
Section~\ref{sec:audit} --- audit the implementation --- is insufficient on its
own.

protocols with the same predicate over the same $31{,}743$ candidates: generations
consisting of newlines only number $1{,}088$ under \Pcode{} and $\mathbf{0}$ under
\Ppaper{}; widening the predicate to whitespace-or-empty gives $1{,}115$ against

on one side and the wide one on the other.) Of the $2{,}552$ tokens \Pcode{}
classifies as glitchy, $1{,}089$ ($42.7\%$) produced pure whitespace. A
representative case is token id $304$ (target \texttt{and}): under \Ppaper{} the
model emits \texttt{"' and '"} and stops; under \Pcode{} it emits ten newlines.
This is consistent with the template's trailing newline placing the model in a
newline-repetition attractor. Because three prompt edits moved together, we
attribute the effect to the wording as a whole rather than to the trailing newline
specifically; isolating the trailing newline requires one further census and is
not yet done.

\paragraph{The disagreement is bidirectional.} $146$ tokens are glitchy under
\Ppaper{} but normal under \Pcode{}, so neither census is a subset of the other.
Any statement of the form ``$N$ tokens stop being glitchy under protocol $X$''
must be paired with the reverse count.

\paragraph{Why this matters.} A repair rate is a fraction of this population. If
the population is set by the probe, then so is every published repair rate, and
rates computed against different populations cannot be compared. This is not
hypothetical: as shown in Section~\ref{sec:why}, \gc{}'s comparison table reports
a \gp{} token count that is \gp{}'s rate rescaled onto \gc{}'s denominator rather
than a quantity \gp{} measured. The practical recommendation is in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: publish the census and the probe verbatim, not just
the rate.

\paragraph{Recall does not reproduce, and the base rate does not account for it.}
Under \Ppaper{} recall is $0.279$. One might suspect this is depressed because our
stricter census makes positives rarer, giving the classifier less signal in its
$10\%$ training sample. We tested that directly by re-scoring under \Pcode{},
whose base rate ($8.0\%$) matches the conditions the papers worked in. Recall

\paragraph{Variance is substantial and is never reported.} Under \Ppaper{} recall
across three seeds spans $0.174$--$0.335$; the only difference between those runs
is which random $10\%$ of the vocabulary trained the classifier. The interval is
much tighter under the higher base rate ($0.382$--$0.417$), which is itself
informative: the method's stability depends on how many positives its sample
happens to contain. A single reported number cannot convey this.

\paragraph{On the ``$100\%$ precision'' claim --- a correction to our own earlier
framing.} \gp{}'s reported $100\%$ precision is true \emph{by construction} rather
than empirically: post-validation admits a token to the glitch set precisely when
it fails the repetition test, so any evaluation against that same oracle returns
$100\%$ by definition. To test whether the claim carries information beyond its
own definition, we scored the detector against an independently generated census.
Precision was $99.7\%$ under both protocols ($2$ and $8$ false positives
respectively across three seeds; per-seed counts $1,0,1$ and $3,4,1$).

The residual is a measurement artefact, and its provenance matters. Our census was
generated with SDPA attention and our detection sweeps with eager attention
(required for extracting attention patterns as features); prompts, batch size,
dtype and decoding were otherwise identical. Under greedy decoding the two kernels
disagree on the generated string for $0.37$--$0.42\%$ of tokens and on the
pass/fail verdict for $0.021\%$ ($2$ of $9{,}522$ sampled). The divergence is
systematic, not stochastic: the same tokens diverged byte-identically across
different batch positions and different sample compositions. Batched fp16
inference was therefore \emph{not} a source of run-to-run non-determinism here,
contrary to our initial write-up.

We further note that in Algorithm 1 the glitch set has two entry points --- tokens
labelled while constructing the SVM training sample, and SVM positives confirmed
by post-validation --- and only the second is re-validated. Under \Ppaper{} all
false positives entered by the first path; post-validation itself admitted none in
$1{,}047$ confirmations.

We therefore do not claim post-validation fails. We claim something narrower and,
for reproducibility, more consequential: \emph{a ``$100\%$ precision'' figure
defined relative to a system's own oracle does not survive contact with an
independently produced ground truth, and the residual is governed by the inference
stack --- attention kernel, numerical precision, decoding path --- none of which
either paper specifies.}

\paragraph{On timing.} We do not compare our wall-clock against the paper's,
because the hardware differs. Our figure is reported only to establish that
detection cost is dominated by brute-force validation passes rather than by the
classifier.

\subsection{RQ3 --- \gp{} repair and its parameterisation}
\label{sec:rq3}

\subsubsection{The published calibration is unrecoverable}

\gp{}'s adaptive adjustment (Equations 9--12) cannot be reconstructed from the
paper. The functional form is given, but $k_1, b_1, k_2, b_2$ are never stated;
the ``adaptive process'' said to derive them is never described; the intervals to
which the ``range restriction'' maps $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are never given; the
``set of default values'' the text says is provided does not appear anywhere; and
no implementation was released. Algorithm 2 compounds this by computing $\beta$
and $\alpha$ from the normal sample alone, although Equations 9--10 require
glitch-token activations, and by treating them as global scalars where
Section 4.2.2 derives them per module.

The consequence is precise: the reported $37.60\%$ repair rate for Mistral cannot
be verified against, or reconstructed from, the paper's description. We do not
claim the method is unimplementable --- only that the published configuration is
unrecoverable, so any reimplementation reports \emph{its own} calibration, not the
authors'. Accordingly we do not report our identity-constant result as the
method's performance; that configuration pins $\alpha \approx 1$, which the sweep
below independently identifies as the null-intervention regime, so it measures the
performance of doing nothing.

\subsubsection{Two under-specified implementation choices dominate the outcome}

Because the calibration is unavailable, we evaluate the \emph{mechanism} directly.
Before doing so, the two under-specified choices from Section~\ref{sec:gp-reimpl}
must be pinned down, because they turn out to matter more than the parameters do.

fixed values ($\alpha = 4$, $\beta = 1.5$).

\paragraph{The corrected statement about $\beta$.} Our earlier claim that
``$\beta$ is inert'' is withdrawn, but the corrected grid supports a narrower
version of it rather than a reversal. The two factors contribute unequally ---
$\alpha$'s marginal spread is $6.13$ percentage points against $\beta$'s $1.20$

$\beta$ axis at $\alpha = 1$ moves repair by $2.4$ percentage points, against the
$6.1$ points $\alpha$ moves on its own. The defensible summary is that
suppression of persistently-silent neurons carries the mechanism, and promotion
contributes materially only in the regime where suppression is switched off ---
which is not the paper's operating regime.

\paragraph{What the landscape does and does not license.} Our first draft argued
here that the paper's claimed calibration gain was unattainable, reasoning from a
repair rate at $(\alpha,\beta) = (4, 1.5)$ \emph{higher} than the $12.92\%$ the
paper reports. That reasoning used the $20.8\%$ from our pre-correction grid ---
the product-stream, all-positions configuration we classify as our own error
(E1) --- and the corrected grid reverses its direction: at the paper's own fixed
values we measure $6.80\%$, roughly \emph{half} what the paper reports for that
configuration, not more. The inference collapses with its premise and is
withdrawn.

What survives is weaker and is a statement about identifiability rather than
attainability. The paper reports its adaptive variant at $37.60\%$ against its own
fixed-value baseline at $12.92\%$, a $2.91\times$ gain attributed to calibration.
We cannot test that ratio, for a reason the paper creates: as
Table~\ref{tab:implchoice} shows, the fixed-value baseline it is measured against
is not determined by the paper's own description --- our four readings of that one
configuration span $2.23$--$17.41\%$, and the published $12.92\%$ sits inside them.
A ratio between a number we cannot reconstruct and a number whose baseline is
under-determined is not a quantity a reproduction can adjudicate. We therefore
report the mechanism's measured behaviour --- a grid maximum of $7.60\%$ under the
paper's reading, saturating in $\alpha$ --- and decline to convert it into a verdict
on the calibration claim.

With the authors' own hyperparameters the gap is $4.85$ points under \Pcode{}, and
under \Ppaper{} held-out \emph{exceeds} train. Most of what we had attributed to
\gc{} was our own under-trained adapter. \gc{} generalises to unseen glitch tokens
substantially better than we previously reported; this is the one correction in
this study that runs in the original authors' favour, and it is the reason the
audit of Section~\ref{sec:audit} is reported rather than quietly absorbed.

That leaves an apparent shortfall --- $78.11\%$ held-out against a published
$94.80\%$ --- and one obvious alternative explanation for it, namely that our
training still differs from theirs. The next subsection removes that explanation
rather than arguing about it.

\subsubsection{The authors' own adapter, on the authors' own population}
\label{sec:upstream}

\gc{} released its trained adapters. We load the Mistral checkpoint directly,
reproducing their \texttt{LinearWithLoRA} arithmetic
($y = \mathrm{linear}(x) + \lambda \cdot \tfrac{\alpha}{r}\,((x A) B)$, with
$A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d'}$ --- the
transpose of PEFT's convention, which is why the weights are not loaded through
PEFT) and reading rank, scaling and target layers from the checkpoint's own
configuration block rather than ours. Their protocol throughout.

\paragraph{The headline claim reproduces, to within one token.} \gc{} reports
$2{,}407$ repaired of $2{,}539$; we measure $2{,}408$. The control run --- the same
weights with the LoRA branch zeroed --- collapses to $0.21\%$, so the repair is
coming from their parameters and not from the base model. This is a second anchor,
independent of the census anchor of Section~\ref{sec:rq1}: our harness reproduces
both \gc{}'s published token list and its published repair rate. Whatever else
this study says about \gc{}, its central number is real and we could not break it.

\paragraph{Our shortfall was ours.} On the $473$ tokens our own adapter held out,
their adapter scores $93.23\%$ where ours scored $78.11\%$ --- same tokens, same
prompt, same decoding, same harness. The $15$-point difference is therefore a
property of the adapter, which means of our training run. We withdraw the
$78.11\%$-versus-$94.80\%$ comparison as a statement about the method. It is a
statement about our replication of the method's training, and reporting it as the
former would be exactly the error this study criticises elsewhere.

\paragraph{What this cannot settle.} Their adapter trained on their entire
published list, so the $473$-token row is a \emph{training-set} number for them
and says nothing about generalisation. The only genuinely unseen population
available is the $15$ tokens our census finds glitchy that are absent from their
list; their adapter repairs all $15$. That is a positive signal at $n = 15$ and
nothing stronger. Whether \gc{} generalises to unseen glitch tokens therefore
remains open: our held-out experiment is the right design for it, and the adapter
it currently uses is not good enough for the answer to be worth quoting. This is
the clearest remaining piece of work in this study, and it is a few minutes of
training.

\paragraph{The gate does not close, and this defeats the losslessness claim.}
\gc{}'s central design claim is that repair is \emph{lossless by construction}:
``a gating mechanism dynamically controls the activation of these branches based
on the model's input, ensuring precise intervention without disrupting normal
inference behavior.'' The gate is a set-membership test --- $\lambda = 1$ when
\emph{any} token of the input lies in the known glitch set $G$ --- and this is how
the authors' released code implements it
(\texttt{GlitchCleaner.py}: \texttt{any(token.item() in self.glitchtokens ...)}).

The set $G$ is the same object as the census of Section~\ref{sec:rq1}, and it
inherits that section's problem in a form that is no longer merely presentational.
\gc{}'s own published $G$ for this model contains the vocabulary entries
\texttt{and}, \texttt{for}, \texttt{with}, \texttt{by}, \texttt{about},
\texttt{because}, \texttt{using}, \texttt{here}, the apostrophe, \texttt{=} and
\texttt{!} --- $26$ of its $2{,}539$ ids fall below id $1{,}000$, i.e.\ among the
most frequent entries in the vocabulary. Consequently:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The gate opens on ordinary prose.} Over sentences drawn from the two
papers under study, $\lambda = 1$ for $197$ of $343$ ($57.4\%$). The single most
frequent cause is the token \texttt{and}.
\item \textbf{The gate opens on the repair evaluation's own boilerplate.} The
prompt template contains ids $304$ (\texttt{and}) and $464$ (the apostrophe), both
in $G$. So $\lambda = 1$ for every probe regardless of the token under test, and
the gate is vacuous during the headline repair measurement.
\item \textbf{It opened in our own measurement, invisibly.} Under \Pcode{} our
evaluation recorded $\lambda = 1$ for all $975$ examples and $\lambda = 0$ for
none, including all $500$ clean tokens
(\texttt{results/gc/.../gccode/eval.json}, \texttt{gate\_stats}). The $99.6\%$
``gated'' cell of Table~\ref{tab:gc} is therefore identical to the forced-on cell
by construction, not by coincidence. Only the \Ppaper{} row measures a closed
gate, and it does so because \emph{our} stricter census happens to exclude
\texttt{and} and the apostrophe --- an accident of our probe, not a property of
the method.
\item \textbf{It never closed for the authors' own adapter either.} Across all
$6{,}079$ examples of Section~\ref{sec:upstream} --- their weights, their
published $G$, their template, their membership rule --- the gate closed
\emph{zero} times. So this is not a property of our reimplementation.
\end{itemize}

The consequence for the paper is specific and, we think, the most decision-relevant
result in this study. \gc{}'s losslessness is not delivered by the mechanism the
paper credits. On ordinary input the adapter is active, so any preservation of
capability is an empirical property of a small, narrowly-trained LoRA --- not a
mathematical guarantee that the base model is what runs. The paper's own Table 4
is consistent with this and, read carefully, demonstrates it: if $\lambda$ were
$0$ on benchmark prompts the \gc{} column would be \emph{bit-identical} to the
original-model column, and it is not (Gemma-2b-it MMLU $38.14 \rightarrow 40.27$;
Llama-2 GSM8K $23.05 \rightarrow 23.88$). Those deltas are only possible if the
adapter was active. We therefore do not dispute that \gc{} preserves capability ---
their evidence supports it and ours is consistent with it. We dispute the stated
reason, and note that ``lossless by construction'' and ``lossless in our
measurements'' warrant different amounts of confidence when the method is deployed
against a different $G$.

\paragraph{Coverage is bounded by a detector \gc{} does not provide.} A glitch
token \emph{not} in $G$ receives $\lambda = 0$ and therefore no repair, by
construction. \gc{} is a method for repairing an enumerated list, and its coverage
in deployment is bounded by the completeness of whatever detection step produced
that list --- a step \gc{} does not perform. Composing our two measurements: the
best detection recall we could reproduce is $0.404$
(Section~\ref{sec:rq2}) and held-out repair is $0.781$ under the same protocol, so
an end-to-end pipeline built from these two published components would repair
roughly $0.404 \times 0.781 \approx 32\%$ of the glitch population, against a
headline of $94.80\%$. That composition assumes independence between the two
stages and inherits every caveat of both, so it is an order-of-magnitude statement
rather than a measurement; we report it because it is the number a practitioner
actually needs and neither paper computes it.

\subsection{RQ5 --- Inference cost}
\label{sec:rq5}

\textbf{We withdraw this measurement and report no inference-speed result.} The
reason is a defect in our own instrumentation, and it is worth stating because it
is the kind of error the rest of this document exists to catch.

Our two speed runs disagreed sharply --- the first gave \gp{} hooks at $0.63\times$
and the \gc{} adapter at $0.56\times$ base, the second gave both at $\approx
0.99\times$ --- and we attributed the reversal to a configuration change, namely
that the first run applied the repair correction at every decoding step where the
second applied it only at the token's own position. Re-reading the code
falsifies that account: \texttt{scripts/run\_speed.py} constructs the hook object
without calling \texttt{bind\_position()}, so the position index falls back to
$-1$ and the correction fires at every decoding step in \emph{both} runs. The file
is unmodified since the repository's first commit, so no configuration change
occurred. The cause of a $1.6\times$ swing therefore remains unexplained, and the
same script measures the \gc{} adapter with no gate attached despite our having
reported that variant as gated.

Publishing either number would mean publishing a quantity we cannot account for.
The instrument is being repaired and each variant re-measured in a fresh process
with controlled ordering; until then this section reports nothing.

Two points nevertheless frame the eventual measurement, and neither depends on it.
First, only ratios within our own environment will be meaningful, since \gc{}'s
published table was measured on different hardware and a different model. Second,
the gate does not exempt \gc{} from overhead: because $\lambda$ multiplies a branch
that is still computed, the adapter's arithmetic cost is paid on clean inputs as
well as glitchy ones --- true of the authors' implementation and of ours. \gc{}'s
``negligible impact on inference speed'' is therefore a claim about the size of the
LoRA, not about the gate. We also note that the $11.82$ tokens/s their Table~6
attributes to \gp{} is, by their own description, their own ``simple \gp{}
implementation'' rather than the original authors' code, which is not released ---
so that row measures an unspecified reimplementation, not a published method.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Which findings are anchored, and which are not}

This distinction governs how strongly each result may be stated, so it is made
explicit rather than left to the reader.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Anchored.} Everything concerning \gc{}. Our census under \Pcode{}
reproduces $2{,}537$ of their $2{,}539$ published tokens, and their released code
allowed component-by-component matching of the adapter, the training targets and
the optimisation settings. Disagreements here are informative.
\item \textbf{Not anchored.} Everything concerning \gp{}. No code was released,
and three specifications are incomplete (the adaptive constants; the adjusted
tensor; the affected positions), two of which we show change the outcome by an
order of magnitude. A disagreement between our measurement and its claim therefore
cannot be attributed with confidence, and is reported as a failure to reproduce
from the published description --- a legitimate finding about reproducibility, but
not a refutation of the method.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Construct validity: the correctness oracle is imperfect}

Both papers, and this study, judge repetition by asking whether the target string
appears in the output. This is weak in two identifiable cases: tokens whose
printable form is empty, for which the test is trivially satisfied, and very short
tokens, where incidental occurrence is plausible. In our candidate set $32$ tokens
are whitespace-only and $3{,}535$ have a stripped length of at most one character
(a further $3{,}503$ once the whitespace-only ones are excluded). Concretely,
$3{,}253$ of the $30{,}755$ verdicts of ``normal'' under \Ppaper{} --- $10.6\%$ ---
rest on a match of at most one character, which could occur incidentally.
The defect is shared with the authors' released code, but sharing it is not a
defence: some fraction of every census in this literature, ours included, is
likely mislabelled for short tokens. We regard this as the most significant
unaddressed construct-validity threat, and note that it is orthogonal to the
protocol sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:rq1} --- fixing the oracle would not
close that gap, since the match rule accounts for only $0.9\%$ of it.

\subsection{Internal validity}

One model; one adapter seed for \gc{}; and a repair evaluation that consumes an
oracle glitch set rather than \gp{}'s own detector output. The last is deliberate
--- it isolates repair capability from detection error --- but it means the two
stages are evaluated separately rather than as the end-to-end pipeline the paper
describes. Detection errors would compound with repair errors in deployment, so
our repair figures are optimistic relative to a full pipeline; the composed bound
in Section~\ref{sec:rq4} is the relevant order of magnitude.

We should be straightforward about the single \gc{} seed: an earlier draft
attributed it to training cost, and that was not true. One adapter trains in
$149$ seconds under \Ppaper{} and $428$ under \Pcode{}
(\texttt{train\_meta.json}), so three seeds is a matter of minutes. It is a gap in
the work, not a constraint on it. The same applies to the three detection seeds at
$211$\,s each. A study whose criticism of both papers is that they under-report
variance should not offer an unreal budget as its own excuse.

\subsection{External validity}

All results are for one 7B model. The papers' headline figures are cross-model
averages, and a single model cannot refute an average; our results are stated as
claims about this model, and where we compare against a published number we use
that paper's \emph{Mistral-specific} figure rather than its average. The
infrastructure is model-agnostic, so extension is a matter of compute.

\subsection{Conclusion validity}

Three seeds bound variance only loosely. Where a difference is small relative to
the observed spread we say so rather than reporting it as a finding. The
$\alpha$/$\beta$ grid is single-seed at $500$ tokens per cell, so individual cell
contrasts of a few tokens are within noise; only the marginal structure is
interpreted.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Recommendations for this literature}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Publish the census and the probe, not just the rate.} The most
consequential result here is that the population a repair rate is a fraction of is
set by the prompt used to find it, and that $99\%$ of a $2.5\times$ difference in
that population comes from wording alone. A paper reporting a repair rate without
its census and its verbatim probe is not reporting a reproducible quantity. The
same applies to reporting a competitor's rate: rescaling it onto one's own
denominator produces a number the competitor never measured.
\item \textbf{Evaluate learned repair on held-out tokens.} A method trained on an
enumerated list and evaluated on that same list reports a quantity that includes
memorisation. The split costs nothing and changes the interpretation.
\item \textbf{Report collateral damage.} Neither paper states what its repair does
to normal tokens. It is cheap to measure and is the natural counterweight to a
repair rate.
\item \textbf{Specify the intervention completely.} For \gp{}, the withheld
constants are the visible gap, but the unstated tensor and unstated positions
change the outcome by an order of magnitude and are invisible to a reader. A
method description should be sufficient to determine what the code does.
\item \textbf{Specify the inference stack.} Attention kernel, precision and
decoding path change the oracle's verdict on a measurable fraction of tokens.
Where a claim is $100\%$ by construction, that should be stated as a definition
rather than presented as a measurement.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{On our own errors}

Two of this study's implementation errors (E1, E2) would have produced
publishable-looking but wrong conclusions had they not been caught, and three of
our stated conclusions were weakened or re-caused by adjudication against the
stored artefacts --- in one case (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}) reversing the causal
attribution entirely while strengthening the underlying claim.

We report this because a benchmark's authority rests on its auditability. The
safeguard that actually worked was not care but \emph{structure}: retained raw
generations made the factorial decomposition possible after the fact; recorded
provenance exposed artefacts attributed to impossible commits; a configuration
fingerprint caught a stale checkpoint masquerading as a fresh result; and an
external reviewer instructed to disbelieve the documentation found what internal
review had not. We would expect any comparable study without these properties to
contain comparable errors, undetected.

We would add one qualification that our own later corrections forced on us. The
first audit found errors in the \emph{code}. The two corrections recorded in
Section~\ref{sec:withdrawn} --- the losslessness result and the speed result ---
are errors in the \emph{reporting}: in both cases the code did something
defensible and the prose described something else, and in both cases every number
involved was real, stored, and reproducible. Auditing an implementation does not
catch these. What caught them was re-reading each stored artefact against the
sentence written about it, which is a different and less glamorous discipline.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{For a reader deciding what to believe.} \gc{}'s headline repair rate is
real: loading the authors' own released adapter reproduces it to within one token
($2{,}408$ against $2{,}407$ of $2{,}539$). We tried twice to find a shortfall in
it --- first through a memorisation argument, then through a held-out comparison
--- and both times the shortfall turned out to be in our replication of their
training rather than in their claim. It is measured on the training population,
which still means it is not a generalisation estimate, and the one small unseen
population we could construct ($n = 15$) is too small to settle that. \gp{}'s
repair mechanism works but weakly, and its published configuration cannot be
reconstructed at all; its $37.60\%$ comes from a calibration the paper does not
disclose, and its $12.92\%$ baseline is under-determined by its own description.

\paragraph{For a reader deciding what to deploy.} Three things bound what either
method delivers, and none is visible from the papers. First, coverage is bounded
by detection: \gc{} repairs only tokens on a list it is given, and the best
detection recall we could reproduce is $0.404$, so a pipeline assembled from these
two published components covers roughly a third of the population, not
$94.80\%$. Second, the ``lossless'' guarantee is not a guarantee: the gate that
provides it opens on the majority of ordinary text, because the token set it
consults contains common English words. Capability preservation is an empirical
property of a small adapter, which is a weaker and more fragile assurance than the
one advertised, and one that must be re-established for every new glitch set.
Third, the population itself is a property of the probe. Anyone deploying these
methods will build their own glitch set with their own prompt, and a
$2.5\times$ difference in that set is attainable by wording alone.

\paragraph{What we could not settle.} \gp{}'s adaptive calibration is
unrecoverable from its paper, so its principal claim can be neither reproduced nor
refuted --- only reported as unreconstructable. Its fixed-value baseline is
under-determined by its own description, our four defensible readings spanning
$2.23$--$17.41\%$ around its published $12.92\%$. Whether \gc{} generalises to
unseen glitch tokens is still open: the design that answers it is ours, but the
adapter we trained is not good enough for its answer to be worth quoting, and the
authors' adapter has seen every token on their list. And our own inference-speed
measurement was withdrawn rather than published. These are stated as open, because
a reproduction study that converts every gap into a negative finding is not
measuring the methods --- it is measuring its own confidence.

\paragraph{A closing note on direction.} Of the substantive corrections this study
made to its own earlier drafts, most moved \emph{in the original authors' favour}:
the memorisation gap, the held-out shortfall, the losslessness measurement, the
inference-speed penalty, and the claim that \gp{}'s calibration gain was
unattainable. What survived in the other direction is narrower and more specific
--- the census is a property of the probe, the competitor counts are rescaled
rather than quoted, \gp{}'s repair is under-specified to the point of being
unfalsifiable, and \gc{}'s losslessness is credited to the wrong mechanism. We
think that asymmetry is the most useful thing to report about how reproduction
studies go wrong: the easy failure mode is not missing an error, it is finding
one that is your own and publishing it as theirs.
